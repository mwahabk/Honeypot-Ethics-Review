─── JUSTIFICATION TEXT (for coding) ───

[DOMAIN]
Value: IoT
Evidence: Section I: Targets "Internet of Things (IoT) cameras" used in consumer, enterprise, and military settings like "home security" and "smart cities," which are categorized as Cyber-Physical Systems (CPS) in the project

[INTERACTION LEVEL]
Evidence: Abstract: Repeatedly identified as a "high-interaction IoT camera honeypot"

[THREAT MODEL]
Value: IoT camera attackers and botnets
Evidence: Section III & IV-B: Targets attackers who "locate IoT cameras through port scanning tools" to "steal sensitive private data" or "install malware"
. Deployment captured the "Mozi botnet"

[IRB / ETHICS COMMITTEE]
Evidence: A review of the manuscript confirms there is no mention of an Institutional Review Board, ethics committee, or human subject protocols

[CONSENT MODEL]
Evidence: Section I, IV-B, & IV-B-1: The researchers deployed HoneyCam across "Virginia, Tokyo and Paris" on the "public Internet" where it received "unsolicited connections from 24120 distinct remote IP addresses"
. Because the authors provide no evidence of warning banners or disclosure, they rely on the "unsolicited" nature of the interaction to justify data collection without formal consent.

[DATA TYPES COLLECTED]
Evidence: Section IV-A & IV-B-1: The system records "attacker information such as IP address, video watching time and camera control commands" and captured "Mozi malware executable"

[PROTOCOLS & PORTS]
Evidence: Section III-A-1: Specifically emulates a real IoT camera by opening ports 80, 8080, 8000, 554, and 1935 to support standard camera protocols

[DATA SENSITIVITY]
Value: High
Evidence: Section IV-B-1: Collects data from "24,120 distinct remote IP addresses" and analyzes "geographic location" and "attack traces" including specific botnet infection attempts

[DATA MINIMIZATION]
Value: Partially
Evidence: Section I & III: Uses "prerecord 360° video" instead of real cameras to avoid exposing "sensitive private data" of real surveillance environments while maintaining realism

[DATA RETENTION POLICY]
Evidence: Section III-A-2: Mentions data is stored in a "local MySQL database" and backed up via "rsync," but provides no statement on storage duration or deletion

[DATA SHARING]
Value: Not mentioned
Evidence: While basic statistics are provided, there is no mention of a public release for the dataset or the captured malware traces

[JURISDICTION / LEGAL]
Evidence: Section IV-B:
Despite deploying global nodes in "Virginia, Tokyo and Paris," there is no mention of GDPR, legal compliance, or jurisdiction

[CONTAINMENT & HARM]
Evidence: Section III-A: The system uses a "frontend honeypot" (virtual server) as a "decoy system" that is "isolated from real backend systems"
. Harm and privacy violations are prevented by using "prerecord 360° video" instead of live cameras to avoid exposing sensitive private data of real surveillance environments (such as a house owner’s presence) while maintaining high-interaction realism for the attacker
.

[DECEPTION FRAMING]
Evidence: Section I & IV-B-2: Deception is a technical goal to "realistically emulates an IoT camera" and make attackers believe they are "interacting with a real camera"

[RESEARCHER RESPONSIBILITY]
Value: Implicit
Evidence: Sections I & II: Framing is purely defensive, focused on "improve the security of IoT camera systems" and using video as a "bait for deception",.
 
By using pre-recorded 360° video, the authors technically mitigate the privacy risks of real camera deployment.
 However, as identified in the project's meta-analysis, this represents the general pattern of "ethics-by-containment," where technical safety is used as a total substitute for formal ethical oversight or IRB involvement.

[ETHICAL GAPS]
Value: Structural Ethical Vacuum
Evidence: Section IV-B-1: Represents the project's meta-finding where technical safety (simulated video) replaces normative reasoning. 
Deploys against "24120" IPs without IRB or governance oversight.

. Unlike HoneySat, it lacks formal governance despite its high interaction
