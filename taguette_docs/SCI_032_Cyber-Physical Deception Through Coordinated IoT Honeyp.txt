─── JUSTIFICATION TEXT (for coding) ───

[DOMAIN]
Value: CPS
Evidence: The paper proposes a Cyber-Physical Deception System (CPDS) that coordinates multiple IoT honeypots to mimic cyber-physical dependencies among IoT devices, making honeypots undetectable by cross-layer attackers. It is explicitly a honeypot design and deployment paper for IoT/CPS environments.

[INTERACTION LEVEL]
Value: Medium
Evidence: CPDS coordinates multiple honeypots with modeled vulnerabilities and physical/network dependency rules. Attackers can interact with services and receive consistent cross-layer responses  higher than low-interaction but not full system access, making this medium interaction.

[THREAT MODEL]
Value: IoT device attackers operating across both cyber (network exploitation) and physical (device state verification) domains, capable of detecting inconsistencies among honeypots through multi-source attacks.
Evidence: Abstract: "attackers can easily detect inconsistencies among the honeypots after launching attacks from multiple sources, spanning both cyber and physical domains, to verify device status."

[IRB / ETHICS COMMITTEE]
Value: Yes
Evidence: Section 8 (Ethics Considerations): "Since the real demo experiment in Section 5.2 involves user participation based on surveys, it has been approved by our Institutional Review Board (IRB). Participants are fully informed about the purpose of the experiments. Apart from this aspect, this research does not involve any personal data, sensitive information, or vulnerability discovery and therefore does not raise specific ethical concerns."

[CONSENT MODEL]
Value: Mixed
Evidence: IRB-approved informed consent applies only to the human survey participants (Section 5.2: "Participants are fully informed about the purpose of the experiments"). 

The online honeypot deployment against real internet attackers 
(Section 5.1) remains implicit/no-consent, as attackers are not notified:

Section 5.1: "we deploy virtual servers running the designated honeypots on the public internet across three geographic locations: the United States (North Virginia), France (Paris), and Japan (Tokyo)."


Section 3.2.2: "we have implemented our attack trace collection system and deployed on the public Internet for a duration of two months starting from Sep. 2023, where the backend infrastructure comprises a server and various IoT devices."


Section 3.2.1: "The system interacts with the attacker by forwarding the received packet to one of the IoT devices... This process continues until the attacker exploits certain vulnerability of the target device or stops interacting with the IoT device."

[DATA TYPES COLLECTED]
Value: Attack traces, attacker IP addresses, CVE exploit attempts, device interaction logs, physical state queries
Evidence: Paper describes collecting and analyzing "attack traces" to model device vulnerabilities. CVE identifiers (CVE-2020-12109, CVE-2021-44402, CVE-2018-3928, CVE-2019-3949, CVE-2020-35577) are referenced as attack data collected.

Table 3 gives precise per-device statistics: the router honeypot (HoneyIoT+Cowrie) recorded 97,894 attack sessions from 6,270 unique attacker IPs, detecting 963 C&C servers and collecting 870 malware samples; the enhanced HoneyCam recorded 48,532 sessions from 4,632 IPs. Malware was submitted to VirusTotal for classification (Section 5.1.2).

[PROTOCOLS & PORTS]
Value: UPnP, device-specific IoT protocols; ports vary by emulated device (smart plug, smart bulb, router, camera)
Evidence: Related work section references U-POT (UPnP protocol), and the system emulates protocol-level responses for various IoT device types. Specific ports depend on emulated device class.

[DATA SENSITIVITY]
Value: Medium
Evidence: Data includes attacker IPs and CVE-specific exploit traces. No personal benign user data is described. Medium sensitivity given identifiable attacker network metadata and potential cross-domain device state information.

[DATA MINIMIZATION]
Value: Yes (partial)
Evidence: Section 5.1.1: "We then preprocess the pre-recorded video using Insta studio to ensure no sensitive information remains." Section 5.2: "To ensure privacy, we review all prepared video streams in HoneyCam and set the resolution of the live video stream to 360p."

[DATA RETENTION POLICY]
Value: Not discussed
Evidence: No data retention policy, deletion timeline, or archival policy mentioned in the paper.

[DATA SHARING]
Value: Source code publicly released (Zenodo)
Evidence: Section 9 (Compliance with Open Science Policy): "This work complies with the newly introduced Open Science Policy by USENIX. All code will be made publicly available... The URL of the Zenodo repository is https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.1472979"

[JURISDICTION / LEGAL]
Value: Not discussed
Evidence: No jurisdiction analysis, GDPR compliance discussion, or legal framing appears in any section of the paper despite deployment facing real-world IoT attackers.

[CONTAINMENT & HARM]
Value: Honeypot isolation implied; physical process emulated synthetically (no real IoT devices attached to physical processes); Prolog rule-based dependency enforcement ensures consistent fake state.
Evidence: The system models physical dependencies as Prolog rules and coordinates honeypot responses to ensure cross-layer consistency real physical processes are not connected, preventing actual harm. Containment is technical by design.

[DECEPTION FRAMING]
Value: Purely technical
Evidence: Deception is discussed entirely as a technical coordination problem ("cross-layer consistency," "cyber-physical dependency emulation"). No ethical framing of deception, attacker engagement, or dual-use considerations appears.

[RESEARCHER RESPONSIBILITY]
Value: Not discussed
Evidence: No researcher responsibility statements, ethical guidelines, or responsible disclosure discussion appear anywhere in the paper.

[ETHICAL GAPS]
Value: No IRB; no consent model; no data governance; no legal/jurisdiction discussion despite real-world deployment. Dual-use risk of coordinated deception infrastructure not acknowledged.
Evidence: No ethics section exists. Despite deployment facing real-world IoT attackers with logging of attacker IPs and CVE exploit attempts, there is no discussion of data protection, responsible disclosure, or regulatory compliance.

[RELEVANCE TO PROJECT]
Value: Highly relevant to HoneySat/Ethics framework and Digital Twin: directly addresses cyber-physical honeypot coordination in IoT/CPS environments, the closest parallel to satellite/space CPS honeypot design. Demonstrates need for ethical framework governing multi-layer deception in CPS contexts.
Evidence: CPDS addresses precisely the problem HoneySat faces: maintaining cross-layer consistency in a cyber-physical honeypot to prevent detection. The absence of ethical governance mirrors the gap our framework targets. Published at USENIX Security 2025 high-impact, recent, directly relevant.

[DEPLOYMENT]
Value: Yes (real-world IoT attacker deployment)
Evidence: The system is designed to face real-world IoT attackers and collects actual attack traces and CVE exploit data. The paper describes deployment against real attacker traffic, not just controlled lab evaluation.

Section 5.1.1: deployed "across three geographic locations: the United States (North Virginia), France (Paris), and Japan (Tokyo)." Section 3.2.2 confirms the attack trace collection system was "deployed on the public Internet for a duration of two months starting from Sep. 2023."
